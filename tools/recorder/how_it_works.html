How Sound Along times words

What happens between a volunteer's microphone and a highlighted word, using a real first take of "The Ants and the Grasshopper". The numbers are from that recording.

Two tracks, and one rule that keeps them honest

Audio and word lists travel side by side. The word list along the way only decides what to show the reader and where to look for pauses. The times children see are made fresh from the finished file, so a mistake in an earlier list can't shift a highlight.

How gaps are measured: sound, not the recognizer

The audio is split into 10 ms slices and each gets a loudness. One line is set per recording: halfway between room and voice, and at least 20 dB below the voice. A pause is a run of slices below it. This matters because the recognizer's times can be far off: it said this "What," began at 18.76 s, and the voice begins at 19.67 s.

Where the numbers live

A word's timing is three numbers in a plain file: start, end, and a flag. Three files carry it from recognizer to screen.

How a reread joins

The tool replaces old audio between two pauses with new audio between two pauses, sized so the silences around the sentence stay as long as they were. Across four real joins, the pauses stayed within about 20 ms.

Do we need a GPU? Only where the listening runs

A volunteer's device only records. Listening runs on whatever computer serves the page. Moving to Amazon changes one box, so the choice is narrower than it looks.

What isn't proven

  • No person has checked word times against the sound. Every timing figure compares one method with another.
  • One reader, one laptop microphone, three false starts. Noisy rooms are untested beyond added steady noise.
  • Where the recognizer skips words, their times are spread evenly and can be off by over a second.
  • Whether cuts and joins sound natural rests on one person's listening so far. A blind test is the next measure: can a stranger find the edits?
